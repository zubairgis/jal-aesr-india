01 · Research case

India does not face one agricultural water-security problem

Similar climate variability can produce fundamentally different agricultural consequences depending on whether crop-water demand is buffered by groundwater, exposed directly to rainfall, or supported by stored and routed surface water.

P1 · Groundwater-buffered

Karnal, Haryana

Groundwater access can sustain irrigation during rainfall deficits while transferring pressure to aquifer storage and recovery.

Climate variability → aquifer storage/access → pumping → crop-stage water supply
P2 · Rainfall-dependent

Yavatmal, Maharashtra

Where irrigation buffering is limited, agricultural exposure responds more directly to monsoon timing, dry spells and rainfall reliability.

Climate variability → rainfall timing/dry spells → crop-stage water supply
P3 · Surface-water-buffered

Mettur–Cauvery, Tamil Nadu

Reservoirs, rivers, tanks and canals store or route water through time, partially decoupling agricultural supply from immediate local rainfall.

Climate variability → stored/routed surface water → irrigation → crop-stage supply

Central proposition

Agricultural water security depends not only on how much water exists, but on where it is stored, how farmers can access it, and whether it is available when crops actually require it.

03 · Preliminary proof of need

Existing observations already reveal different water-source behaviours

The pilot deliberately uses simple in-situ observations rather than reproducing the full proposed national analysis. Its purpose is to demonstrate why rainfall, groundwater and surface water must be interpreted together with agricultural demand timing.

What the pilot establishes

Meteorological water availability and agricultural water availability are not equivalent. A source-aware, crop-timed framework is therefore required before irrigation security can be diagnosed nationally.

The pilot does not claim national representativeness, causal attribution, a common physical stress scale, complete temporal coverage at every site, or direct groundwater measurement by NISAR.

STATE

AgroWaterState

Surface water, soil moisture, groundwater, recharge, irrigation dependence and crop-water demand are modelled as connected but non-equivalent components.

SECURITY

Irrigation security

Capacity of the accessible water system to meet crop-water requirements during critical growth stages with acceptable reliability and without persistent groundwater depletion.

VALIDATION

Observation first

Groundwater state is anchored to monitored records. NISAR supports near-surface moisture, inundation, agricultural response and physically appropriate deformation analysis—not direct groundwater storage measurement.

05 · Research objectives

Six linked objectives from national data harmonisation to operational water intelligence

  1. O1

    National data cube

    Build a harmonised surface-water–groundwater–irrigation data architecture across India’s agro-ecological regions.

  2. O2

    Surface-water reliability

    Quantify seasonal availability, persistence, storage and routing of surface-water resources relevant to agriculture.

  3. O3

    Soil-moisture and irrigation response

    Use NISAR, Indian EO missions and complementary sensors to characterise near-surface moisture and agricultural response.

  4. O4

    Groundwater stress and recovery

    Quantify monitored groundwater stress, recovery and recharge behaviour, with InSAR only where physically appropriate.

  5. O5

    Crop-stage sufficiency

    Evaluate water-source coupling, irrigation dependence and crop-stage water sufficiency across contrasting AESRs.

  6. O6

    National observatory

    Develop and independently validate agricultural water-resilience classes and the Digital Water Observatory.
